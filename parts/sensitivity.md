# Part VI — What the answer rests on

> Given a wide interval, what is the one thing to go and do about it?

An interval describes a problem without saying what to do about it. This part answers that, and
then shows the limit of everything the book has built.

**[ch19 · Which input to go and measure](#which-input-is-the-answer)** ranks the inputs by how far
each one moves the answer. Then it answers the question a ranking raises: what measuring the top
one would buy, as an upper bound. Two of its results would carry the chapter on their own. The
book's most carefully measured constants buy nothing, and in the observability model the input
that ties for first cannot be measured at all.

**[ch20 · The missing node](#the-missing-node)** is the error no technique in this book can rank,
because it is not in the list. A model can be wrong about a number or wrong about its shape.
Everything in Part IV and [ch19](#which-input-is-the-answer) is about the first kind of error; this
chapter is about the second. Around a model of the wrong shape, the machinery does worse than miss
the error: it produces an interval settled around the wrong answer, and the settling looks rigorous.

The order matters. A reader who does [ch19](#which-input-is-the-answer) without
[ch20](#the-missing-node) has learned to optimise a list without asking whether the list is
complete.
